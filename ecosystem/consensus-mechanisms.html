CONSENSUS MECHANISMS

How Blockchain Networks Agree

Proof of Work, Proof of Stake, Byzantine Fault Tolerance and the principles that allow independent participants to coordinate a shared digital ledger.

INTRODUCTION

A blockchain network has no requirement that every participant trust a central operator to maintain the ledger. Instead, independent computers receive information, check it against protocol rules and participate in a process that determines which valid transactions become part of the network's accepted history. This coordination problem is commonly discussed under the broad heading of consensus.

Consensus is not a single algorithm or a synonym for mining or staking. It is a system of rules, communication procedures, incentives and security assumptions. Depending on the network, it may determine who can propose a block, how participants evaluate competing histories, how votes are counted, when a decision becomes final and what happens when some participants fail or behave dishonestly.

This study examines several major approaches: Nakamoto-style consensus and Proof of Work; Proof of Stake systems; Byzantine Fault Tolerant protocols; and Crash Fault Tolerant algorithms such as Raft. It also compares their assumptions and limitations. The purpose is not to declare one model universally superior, but to understand what each model is designed to achieve.

01. The Consensus Problem

1.1 A Shared Ledger Without a Central Referee

Imagine that thousands of computers maintain copies of the same transaction history. A participant broadcasts a transaction, but messages do not arrive everywhere at the same instant. Some computers may be offline. Others may receive conflicting proposals. A malicious participant may send different information to different peers or attempt to spend the same digital asset twice.

If each computer simply accepted the first message it received, different parts of the network could develop different records. The system therefore needs rules that let participants check proposed updates and converge on a consistent history despite delays, failures and adversarial behaviour.

This is the central coordination challenge: how can independent participants agree on one ordered history or one state transition without relying on a single trusted decision-maker?

1.2 The Byzantine Generals Problem

The Byzantine Generals Problem is a model of agreement in which participants communicate but some may send conflicting, misleading or arbitrary messages. The name comes from a thought experiment about generals who must coordinate an action while some messengers or generals may be traitors. In distributed computing, the analogy describes computers that may fail in unpredictable ways or deliberately act against the protocol.

Blockchains face a related problem. A network cannot assume that every node is honest simply because it is connected. It must define how many faulty or malicious participants it can tolerate, how their influence is measured, and what happens when the network cannot safely reach agreement.

Important distinction

Consensus does not mean that every computer has identical information at every instant. Distributed networks experience message delays and temporary differences. The protocol aims to ensure that honest participants follow compatible rules and, under its stated assumptions, converge on an agreed result.

1.3 Open and Permissioned Participation

In a permissioned system, membership is controlled: participants may be known organisations or approved servers. This can make it practical to use voting protocols among a defined committee. In a permissionless public network, anyone may attempt to participate. The system must therefore address Sybil attacks, in which one actor creates many identities to gain disproportionate influence.

Proof of Work and Proof of Stake are two prominent ways to make influence costly in an open network. They do not simply count identities. Instead, they connect influence to a resource: computational work in PoW or economic stake in PoS. Permissioned systems may use identity, membership rules and voting committees instead.

02. What a Consensus Mechanism Includes

2.1 More Than a Block-Production Method

The phrase “consensus mechanism” is often used informally to mean Proof of Work or Proof of Stake. Technically, these are only parts of a broader design. A complete consensus system can include:

  • Sybil-resistance: a method for preventing an attacker from gaining influence merely by creating many identities.
  • Proposer or leader selection: rules determining who may propose the next block or value.
  • Transaction and block validation: rules that determine whether proposed data is valid.
  • Fork choice or voting: rules for resolving competing proposals or deciding which history is accepted.
  • Finality: rules or thresholds that determine when a decision can no longer be reverted under the protocol's assumptions.
  • Incentives and penalties: rewards, fees, collateral, slashing or other mechanisms that influence participant behaviour.
  • Network assumptions: expectations about message delivery, connectivity, synchrony and participant availability.

These components interact. A system may use stake to select or weight validators, a voting protocol to confirm blocks and a separate fork-choice rule to select the current chain head. Describing the whole design with only one label can hide important details.

2.2 Validation Is Not the Same as Consensus

Every full node can independently check whether a transaction or block follows the protocol's validity rules. Consensus addresses a different question: among the valid proposals and histories that may exist, which ordering or state should the network treat as canonical?

A block producer cannot make an invalid transaction valid simply by proposing it. Nodes that enforce the protocol rules reject invalid data even if it was produced by a powerful miner or a highly weighted validator. Consensus coordinates the ordering and acceptance of valid state transitions; it does not replace independent verification.

2.3 Consensus and Execution

In programmable networks, execution determines the result of transactions: balances change, smart contracts run and application state is updated. Consensus coordinates the ordering and acceptance of those operations. Some architectures separate execution and consensus into distinct clients or layers; others integrate these responsibilities more tightly. The distinction is useful even when one software implementation performs both tasks.

03. Safety, Liveness and Fault Models

3.1 Safety: Nothing Contradictory Is Final

Safety means that the protocol does not allow conflicting decisions to become final under its stated assumptions. In a replicated ledger, safety is concerned with preventing honest participants from finalising incompatible histories or applying different commands in the same agreed position.

3.2 Liveness: The System Can Make Progress

Liveness means that valid progress can eventually occur. A network that never accepts a wrong block but also never accepts any block is safe in a narrow sense, yet it is not useful. Consensus designs must balance the ability to keep making progress with the need to avoid contradictory outcomes.

3.3 Crash Faults and Byzantine Faults

A crash fault occurs when a computer stops responding or becomes unavailable. A Byzantine fault is broader: a participant may crash, delay messages, send inconsistent messages, lie, or otherwise behave arbitrarily. Byzantine fault tolerance is therefore a stronger adversarial model than crash fault tolerance.

3.4 Network Timing Assumptions

Distributed protocols must account for uncertain message delays. In a fully asynchronous network, there is no known upper bound on how long a message may take. The FLP impossibility result shows that deterministic consensus cannot guarantee termination in every fully asynchronous execution if even one process may crash. Practical systems address this through assumptions such as partial synchrony, randomized approaches, or by separating safety from timing-dependent liveness.

For example, a protocol may preserve safety regardless of temporary delays but only guarantee progress after communication becomes sufficiently reliable. The precise guarantee depends on the protocol and its model; “fast” or “secure” without those assumptions is not a meaningful technical description.

Two questions to ask about every protocol

1. Under what conditions can honest participants never finalise conflicting results? 2. Under what conditions can they continue to make progress? A sound comparison of consensus systems must answer both questions.

04. Proof of Work

4.1 Computational Work as a Costly Resource

Proof of Work (PoW) uses computational effort to make block production costly. In a typical PoW system, miners repeatedly calculate hashes while changing a nonce or other block data. A valid result must satisfy a target set by the network's difficulty rules. Finding a valid result requires repeated attempts; checking the result is comparatively inexpensive.

This asymmetry is central to PoW: producing a proof requires substantial expected work, while other nodes can verify the proof efficiently. The resource is not a miner's claimed identity or voting preference. It is demonstrated computational work.

4.2 Mining and Block Proposals

Miners assemble candidate blocks from transactions they consider eligible under their node's policy and the protocol rules. They compete to find a valid proof. A miner that finds one broadcasts its block. Other nodes independently check the proof, block structure and transactions. A block that fails validation is rejected regardless of the amount of work claimed for it.

4.3 Difficulty and Incentives

Difficulty adjustment aims to keep block production near a protocol-defined target over time, even as total hash rate changes. Miners incur costs for hardware, electricity, facilities and operations. Protocol rewards and transaction fees can compensate successful miners, although actual profitability varies with market conditions and operating costs.

PoW links influence over block production to the ability to perform computational work. An attacker seeking to reorganise history must compete with the honest network's accumulated work. This does not make attacks mathematically impossible; it changes their cost and probability under assumptions about the distribution of hash power and network conditions.

4.4 Advantages and Limitations

  • Open participation: in permissionless PoW systems, participants can generally contribute hash power without first being admitted to a validator list.
  • Public verifiability: nodes can independently verify proofs and block validity.
  • Resource cost: rewriting history requires substantial computational work and associated expenditure.
  • Energy use: competitive hashing consumes electricity; the scale depends on network design and mining activity.
  • Industrial concentration: economies of scale in hardware, energy procurement and operations can favour large mining operators.
  • Probabilistic settlement: in Nakamoto-style systems, confidence in a transaction generally increases as more work is built on top of its block, rather than appearing as an immediate absolute finality guarantee.

05. Nakamoto Consensus and Bitcoin

5.1 The Contribution of the Bitcoin Design

Bitcoin's 2008 white paper proposed a peer-to-peer electronic cash system that uses a chain of hash-based Proof-of-Work to create a public record of transaction history. Its design addressed double spending without requiring a central institution to maintain the authoritative ledger.

The term Nakamoto consensus is commonly used for the broader combination of PoW-based Sybil resistance, block propagation, independent validation, incentives and a chain-selection rule. It is not simply “the longest chain wins” in a literal count of blocks. For Bitcoin, nodes follow the valid chain with the greatest accumulated proof of work, often described as the chain with the most cumulative work.

5.2 Competing Blocks and Temporary Forks

Two miners may find valid blocks at nearly the same time. Because network messages take time to propagate, different nodes may initially see different blocks first. The network can temporarily contain competing branches. As additional work is added, one branch becomes the preferred valid history under the chain-selection rule, and nodes reorganise if necessary.

This is why a block appearing in a node's view does not automatically mean that its transactions are irreversible. A later chain reorganisation can replace a recent block with a competing valid block. The probability of a successful reorganisation generally decreases as additional work accumulates, assuming the protocol's security conditions hold.

5.3 Confirmation Is Probabilistic

Bitcoin commonly describes a transaction as having a number of confirmations: the block containing it counts as the first confirmation, and each subsequent block adds another. More confirmations normally provide stronger evidence that the transaction will remain in the accepted history. They do not turn the protocol into a system with a universal deterministic finality certificate.

5.4 Security Assumptions

Nakamoto-style security depends on assumptions that include an honest majority of the relevant hash power, sufficiently reliable propagation of information relative to block production, and the security of the underlying cryptographic primitives. The exact formal guarantees depend on the adversary model and network assumptions. A phrase such as “51% attack” is a useful simplification, but real attack capabilities and outcomes depend on details such as timing, mining strategy, transaction selection and the target being attacked.

Bitcoin terminology

Bitcoin nodes validate blocks according to consensus rules. Miners propose blocks and perform PoW, but miners cannot unilaterally change the validity rules followed by independently validating nodes. Mining power and rule enforcement are related but distinct roles.

06. Proof of Stake

6.1 Economic Stake Instead of Competitive Hashing

Proof of Stake (PoS) uses economic stake as a basis for Sybil resistance and, in many designs, validator selection or voting weight. Participants lock or otherwise commit assets under protocol rules. The system selects validators to propose blocks, attest to proposals or vote on the state of the network.

PoS is not one universal algorithm. Networks differ in how stake is deposited, how validators are selected, how votes are weighted, how fork choice works, when blocks become final and what penalties apply. It is therefore more accurate to speak of families of PoS designs than of a single standard PoS mechanism.

6.2 Validators, Proposals and Attestations

A validator may be selected to propose a block for a particular slot or round. Other validators check the proposal and may attest or vote according to the protocol. The network combines these actions with its fork-choice and finality rules to determine the accepted chain and state.

In Ethereum, the proof-of-stake consensus system uses validators, attestations and a fork-choice rule. Its finality mechanism is based on supermajority votes. Ethereum documentation describes the overall system as a combination of components rather than staking alone.

6.3 Rewards, Penalties and Slashing

Validators may receive rewards for correct participation and incur penalties for certain failures or protocol violations. Some PoS systems use slashing to destroy or confiscate part of a validator's stake for objectively provable actions that violate safety rules, such as signing conflicting messages in circumstances defined by the protocol.

Slashing is not a general punishment for every mistake or outage. The conditions are protocol-specific. A validator may also experience ordinary penalties for being offline or failing to perform duties. The exact risks depend on the implementation, client configuration, key management and network rules.

6.4 PoS Security and Its Assumptions

PoS systems typically rely on assumptions about the distribution of stake, the behaviour and independence of validators, the cryptographic security of signatures, and network timing. A stake-weighted majority or supermajority can have substantial influence. The protocol must define what happens if too much stake is controlled by one actor or coordinated group, and how conflicting votes are handled.

6.5 Advantages and Limitations

  • Lower direct energy demand for block production: PoS does not require miners to continuously compete through energy-intensive hashing.
  • Economic accountability: committed stake can align incentives and support penalties for certain provable misbehaviour.
  • Potentially explicit finality: some PoS systems provide finality through validator votes and defined thresholds.
  • Stake concentration: wealth distribution, delegation, custodial services and economies of scale may concentrate influence.
  • Operational complexity: validator keys, software clients, slashing conditions and recovery procedures require careful management.
  • Protocol-specific risks: nothing-at-stake concerns, long-range attacks, weak subjectivity and other issues require design-specific mitigations; they should not be treated as identical across all PoS networks.

07. Delegated and Nominated Stake Models

7.1 Delegated Proof of Stake

Delegated Proof of Stake (DPoS) generally allows token holders to vote for a smaller group of block producers or validators. Those elected participants perform block-production or consensus duties according to the network's rules. The exact voting, rotation, reward and accountability mechanisms differ among implementations.

Delegation can make participation more accessible to holders who do not operate infrastructure themselves. At the same time, it introduces political and governance dynamics: voter participation may be uneven, large holders may exert greater influence, and a limited producer set can create concentration risks.

7.2 Nominated Proof of Stake

Nominated Proof of Stake (NPoS), used in the Polkadot design, involves nominators backing validators with stake. The protocol selects a validator set while taking the distribution of backing and security of the set into account. This is not simply identical to DPoS under another name. The nomination process and validator-election rules are specific to the protocol.

7.3 What the Labels Do and Do Not Tell Us

Names such as DPoS and NPoS provide a first clue about participation and selection, but they do not fully describe security. To evaluate a network, examine the number and independence of active validators, how voting power is distributed, how selection works, what penalties apply, whether users can verify operations, and how governance changes the rules.

08. Byzantine Fault Tolerance

8.1 Agreement Despite Arbitrary Faults

Byzantine Fault Tolerance (BFT) is a family of techniques and protocols designed to maintain agreement even when some participants behave arbitrarily or maliciously. A BFT protocol must address more than silent failures. A faulty participant may send different messages to different peers, propose conflicting values or attempt to disrupt the voting process.

Classical BFT protocols often operate among a known set of participants or a committee. They exchange multiple rounds of messages and use quorum thresholds so that the sets of votes supporting decisions overlap in ways that preserve safety.

8.2 The One-Third Fault Threshold in Classical BFT

Many classical partially synchronous BFT protocols are designed to tolerate fewer than one-third Byzantine participants by voting weight. In a simple equal-weight committee of n validators, this is commonly expressed as tolerating up to f Byzantine validators when n ≥ 3f + 1. The threshold is not a universal law for every protocol: it follows from particular protocol designs and assumptions.

Common committee condition: n ≥ 3f + 1
Meaning: a committee of at least 3f + 1 members can be designed to tolerate up to f Byzantine members under the protocol's assumptions.

8.3 Why Quorum Overlap Matters

Suppose a protocol requires a supermajority of votes before a block becomes final. If two conflicting blocks each needed a sufficiently large quorum, the quorums must overlap. If the number of Byzantine participants is below the protocol's fault threshold, the overlap contains enough honest voting power to prevent honest validators from approving conflicting decisions in violation of the rules.

This is the intuition behind quorum-based safety. The exact proof depends on the quorum size, voting model, validator weights and protocol phases. It is not enough to say “two-thirds agree” without specifying what is being voted on and how the protocol prevents equivocation.

8.4 Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance

Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT), introduced by Miguel Castro and Barbara Liskov, showed how Byzantine state-machine replication could be implemented with practical performance in distributed systems. In simplified form, a leader proposes an operation and replicas exchange messages through protocol phases before committing it. The protocol includes mechanisms for changing the leader when progress stalls or the current leader is suspected of being faulty.

Classical PBFT-style protocols can require substantial communication as committee size grows. This is one reason many modern systems use optimisations, committee selection, signature aggregation or different protocol architectures. The details matter: a protocol inspired by PBFT is not necessarily identical to the original PBFT algorithm.

8.5 Strengths and Limitations

  • Strong agreement properties: quorum-based protocols can provide explicit finality under their assumptions.
  • Fast confirmation in suitable networks: once the required quorum completes the protocol, the decision may be final without waiting for a long sequence of later blocks.
  • Defined membership or committee: many designs assume a known or selected validator set.
  • Communication overhead: repeated message exchange can become expensive as the number of participants increases.
  • Network sensitivity: liveness may depend on eventual synchrony or other timing assumptions.
  • Committee concentration: a small committee may be efficient but requires careful analysis of membership, independence and governance.

09. Practical BFT and Tendermint / CometBFT

9.1 Combining Stake-Based Selection with BFT Voting

Tendermint-style systems illustrate how different components can be combined. A stake-based process can determine the validator set or voting power, while a BFT consensus protocol coordinates proposals and votes among those validators. In this design, Proof of Stake and BFT are not competing labels for the same function: they can serve different roles within one architecture.

9.2 Rounds and Voting Steps

At a high level, Tendermint-style consensus proceeds through rounds. A validator proposes a block, validators exchange prevote and precommit messages, and a block can be committed when the required voting power supports the relevant decision. If a round cannot make progress, the protocol advances to another round, potentially with a different proposer.

The protocol's exact safety and liveness properties depend on its specification and assumptions. Tendermint's design is associated with a less-than-one-third Byzantine voting-power assumption for safety, together with network conditions required for liveness. It is not accurate to claim that any BFT protocol can tolerate one-third or more malicious voting power without qualification.

9.3 CometBFT

CometBFT is the maintained successor in the Tendermint Core software lineage and provides Byzantine Fault Tolerant state-machine replication infrastructure used by blockchain applications. Its documentation and specifications should be consulted for implementation-specific behaviour, including validator voting, block commits and application integration.

9.4 Why This Architecture Matters

The Tendermint / CometBFT family demonstrates a common design pattern: a network can use an economic mechanism to establish who participates and a separate BFT protocol to agree on the next state transition. This separation helps explain why “PoS versus BFT” is often a false comparison. A PoS network may use BFT-style consensus internally.

10. Crash Fault Tolerance and Raft

10.1 A Different Problem: Servers That Stop Responding

Raft is a consensus algorithm for replicated logs in distributed systems. It helps a cluster of servers agree on the sequence of commands to apply, even if some servers crash or become unavailable. It is designed to be understandable and practical, and is often used to explain consensus in distributed databases and infrastructure systems.

Raft is generally classified as Crash Fault Tolerant (CFT), not Byzantine Fault Tolerant. Its model does not attempt to protect against a server that deliberately sends contradictory or malicious protocol messages while appearing to operate correctly.

10.2 Leader Election and Log Replication

Raft organises servers into followers, candidates and a leader. If followers stop receiving heartbeats, an election may begin. A candidate requests votes and becomes leader after receiving a majority. The leader then replicates log entries to followers. Entries are committed when the required majority replication condition is satisfied, subject to Raft's term and log-safety rules.

10.3 Why Raft Is Useful in This Study

Raft helps distinguish the broad distributed-systems concept of consensus from blockchain consensus specifically. A database cluster may know the identities of its servers and assume they follow the protocol unless they crash. A public blockchain must usually address open participation, Sybil resistance and adversaries who may intentionally manipulate messages or economic incentives. The problem settings are therefore different.

Do not confuse CFT and BFT

A system that tolerates two crashed servers in a five-server cluster does not automatically tolerate two malicious servers. Crash tolerance and Byzantine tolerance describe different fault models and cannot be compared by counting failures alone.

11. Finality, Forks and Confirmation

11.1 What Does Finality Mean?

Finality describes the point at which a transaction or state transition is considered irreversible according to a protocol's rules and assumptions. Different networks use the term in different ways. A useful distinction is between probabilistic finality and deterministic or protocol-defined finality.

11.2 Probabilistic Finality

In Nakamoto-style PoW systems, a transaction becomes increasingly difficult to remove as additional blocks and cumulative work build on top of it. Confidence increases with confirmations, but the protocol does not ordinarily issue a single certificate declaring that a particular block can never be reorganised. The risk is reduced, not transformed into mathematical impossibility.

11.3 Protocol-Defined Finality

In many BFT and PoS systems, a block or checkpoint becomes final after a specified voting threshold is reached. Under the protocol's assumptions, conflicting finalised checkpoints cannot both be valid. If the assumptions are violated, for example by an extraordinary concentration of Byzantine voting power or a severe failure of the trust model, the system may halt, require recovery or face a safety incident.

11.4 Confirmation, Inclusion and Settlement

These words should not be treated as interchangeable. A transaction may be received by a node, included in a proposed block, accepted into the current chain, receive additional confirmations, or become final under a protocol's finality rule. Applications and exchanges may apply their own risk policies on top of the protocol's guarantees.

11.5 Fork Choice and Finality Are Related but Distinct

A fork-choice rule helps a node decide which chain head to follow when multiple valid branches exist. A finality gadget or voting protocol may provide stronger guarantees for checkpoints or blocks. Some systems combine both. A description of a consensus mechanism should identify each function rather than assume that one rule performs every task.

12.1 There Is No Single Universal Ranking

A system optimised for open participation may make different trade-offs from a private network operated by known organisations. A design with explicit finality may prioritise quorum communication and validator accountability. A probabilistic chain may favour a simple open participation model and tolerate temporary forks. The correct comparison begins with the system's intended use and threat model.

13. Security, Incentives and Centralisation Risks

13.1 Security Is a System Property

Consensus is only one part of blockchain security. A network can have a well-designed protocol and still be vulnerable through buggy client software, compromised validator keys, poor operational practices, insecure bridges, flawed smart contracts or governance failures. A consensus guarantee applies only within its stated model and does not secure every application built on top of the network.

13.2 Resource and Voting Concentration

PoW influence may concentrate through mining pools, specialised hardware, access to inexpensive energy or control of infrastructure. PoS influence may concentrate through large holders, custodial staking providers, liquid staking arrangements, delegation patterns or validator operators. Committee systems may concentrate power through membership decisions or a small number of independent organisations.

Concentration should be measured carefully. The number of visible nodes is not necessarily the same as the number of independent decision-makers. A thousand nodes controlled by one operator do not provide the same independence as a thousand separately governed participants.

13.3 Incentives and Governance

Protocol rewards, transaction fees, penalties and governance procedures influence participant behaviour. However, economic incentives do not guarantee honest conduct in every circumstance. Participants may have external motives, conflicting business interests or the ability to coordinate. Governance also determines how software upgrades, parameter changes and emergency responses are handled.

13.4 Client Diversity and Implementation Risk

When many independent operators rely on one client implementation, a software defect can affect a large share of the network. Multiple independently developed clients can reduce some correlated risks, although diversity alone does not guarantee correctness. Consensus-critical software requires careful testing, formal reasoning where feasible, security review and disciplined upgrade procedures.

13.5 Censorship and Transaction Ordering

Consensus systems determine how transactions are ordered and included. Validators or miners may have the ability or incentive to delay, exclude or reorder transactions within protocol constraints. The degree of censorship resistance depends on the network's participant distribution, relay paths, proposer selection, governance and the ability of users to reach alternative participants.

14. What Consensus Cannot Solve

Consensus can coordinate a shared digital record, but it cannot independently establish that every real-world statement entered into that record is true. If a transaction depends on an external price feed, delivery confirmation or legal event, the network needs an oracle, trusted data provider or other mechanism to obtain that information. Consensus can agree on the data it receives; it cannot magically verify facts outside its observation model.

Nor does consensus guarantee that a network is decentralised in every meaningful sense, that its software is bug-free, that its governance is fair, that its applications are safe, or that its token has economic value. These are separate questions requiring separate evidence.

A technically sound evaluation should therefore ask: Who can participate? What resource or identity determines influence? What faults are tolerated? What timing assumptions are made? How are conflicting histories resolved? When is a decision final? Who can change the rules? How diverse and independently operated are the clients, validators, miners or authorities?

15. Key Takeaways

  • Consensus is the coordinated process by which distributed participants agree on an accepted ordering or state of a system.
  • Proof of Work and Proof of Stake are important Sybil-resistance and participation mechanisms, but neither label alone describes every component of a complete consensus protocol.
  • Nakamoto-style consensus uses cumulative work and a chain-selection rule; confirmations increase confidence but do not create immediate deterministic finality.
  • Proof of Stake uses economic stake to support validator selection or voting weight; designs differ in their fork-choice, penalties and finality mechanisms.
  • BFT protocols use quorum-based communication to tolerate a defined amount of Byzantine behaviour under explicit assumptions.
  • Raft is a useful example of crash fault tolerant consensus, but it is not designed to tolerate arbitrary malicious participants.
  • Every consensus design makes trade-offs involving security, liveness, decentralisation, performance, energy, complexity and governance.
  • Consensus secures agreement about the data and state governed by the protocol; it does not independently prove the truth of external information or guarantee application safety.

Research perspective

The central question is not simply which consensus mechanism is fastest or cheapest. It is whether the system's assumptions, incentives and failure protections are appropriate for the environment in which it operates—and whether those guarantees can be independently verified.
