BLOCKCHAIN FOUNDATIONS

The Fundamental Principles of Distributed Ledger Technology

What makes a blockchain different from a traditional database? How can computers maintain a shared record without relying on one central administrator? Explore the fundamental components of blockchain technology: distributed ledgers, blocks, nodes, cryptographic hashes, digital signatures and transaction verification.

FOUNDATIONS CRYPTOGRAPHY DISTRIBUTED SYSTEMS DATA INTEGRITY

01. WHAT IS A BLOCKCHAIN?

A blockchain is a type of digital ledger in which records are organised into groups called blocks. These blocks are linked together using cryptographic references, forming an ordered chain of records.

Unlike a conventional database controlled through one central system, a distributed blockchain network can maintain copies of its ledger across multiple computers. These computers are known as nodes.

Participants follow a set of protocol rules that define how transactions are checked, how new blocks are accepted and how the network maintains a shared history.

A blockchain is therefore not simply a database stored on the internet. It combines data structures, cryptographic techniques, network communication and rules for coordinating independent participants.

02. DISTRIBUTED LEDGER TECHNOLOGY

Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT) describes systems in which records are maintained and synchronised across multiple participants or locations.

A distributed ledger does not necessarily have to use a blockchain. Blockchain is one particular way of organising and linking records within a distributed ledger system.

Depending on the network design, participants may have different permissions. Some networks allow broad public participation, while others restrict who can read, submit or validate records.

The distributed structure can reduce dependence on a single record-keeping institution. However, it does not automatically eliminate the need for governance, coordination or trust in software, operators and system rules.

03. BLOCKS AND CHAIN STRUCTURE

A block is a data structure that contains a collection of information. In many blockchain systems, this information includes transactions and metadata used by the network protocol.

A block commonly contains a reference to the preceding block. This reference is based on a cryptographic hash of the previous block's relevant data.

Because each block is linked to its predecessor, changing information in an earlier block would affect its hash and break the expected references in subsequent blocks.

This structure makes unauthorised changes detectable. It does not mean that every blockchain has identical block formats or that changes are technically impossible under every circumstance.

The exact structure of a block depends on the design and protocol of the particular network.

04. NODES AND NETWORK PARTICIPANTS

A node is a computer or software instance participating in a blockchain network. Nodes communicate with other participants and perform functions defined by the protocol.

Depending on the network, a node may receive transactions, share information, verify blocks, maintain ledger data or participate in the process of proposing and confirming new blocks.

Not every node performs every function. Some systems distinguish between full nodes, light clients, block producers and other specialised participants.

A full node generally checks blockchain data against the applicable protocol rules. This independent verification is an important part of distributed network operation.

The responsibilities and technical requirements of nodes differ between blockchain networks.

05. CRYPTOGRAPHIC HASH FUNCTIONS

A cryptographic hash function takes input data and produces an output of a defined format, commonly called a hash or digest.

A secure hash function is designed so that it is computationally difficult to reconstruct the original input from its hash or to find two different inputs that produce the same output.

Even a small change in the input will, with overwhelming probability for a secure hash function, produce a different digest.

Blockchain systems use hashes in several ways, including linking blocks, checking data integrity and organising transaction information.

Hashing is not encryption. A hash function does not provide a method for decrypting the original information.

06. DIGITAL SIGNATURES

Digital signatures are cryptographic mechanisms used to verify that information was authorised by the holder of a corresponding private key and that the signed information has not been modified after signing.

Public-key cryptography uses a mathematically related key pair:

  • Private key: kept secret and used to create a digital signature.
  • Public key: shared with others and used to verify the corresponding signature.

In a blockchain transaction, a digital signature can demonstrate that the transaction was authorised according to the network's cryptographic rules.

A digital signature does not automatically reveal the real-world identity of the person controlling a key. It provides cryptographic evidence of authorisation, not necessarily proof of a legal identity.

The specific signature algorithms and address systems differ between blockchain protocols.

07. TRANSACTION VALIDATION

A blockchain transaction is a structured request to record an operation according to the rules of a particular network.

Before a transaction can become part of the accepted ledger history, it must satisfy the relevant protocol requirements.

Depending on the blockchain, validation may include checks such as:

  • Correct transaction structure and format.
  • Valid digital signatures or authorisation data.
  • Compliance with spending or account rules.
  • Correct execution of applicable protocol conditions.
  • Compliance with network-specific restrictions.

Nodes can independently verify transactions and blocks. Network consensus mechanisms determine how participants coordinate around an accepted history or state.

Transaction validation and consensus are related, but they are not the same process. Validation checks whether data follows the rules. Consensus coordinates agreement about the network's accepted history.

08. IMMUTABILITY AND ITS LIMITS

Blockchain records are often described as immutable. In practice, it is more precise to describe many blockchain systems as tamper-evident and tamper-resistant.

Cryptographic links between blocks make modifications to earlier records detectable. The distributed structure and network rules can make unauthorised changes increasingly difficult as the accepted history develops.

However, immutability is not an absolute guarantee that a ledger can never change. Different systems have different security assumptions, governance structures and mechanisms for handling competing histories.

Network reorganisations, protocol upgrades, software defects, compromised keys and governance decisions can affect how a blockchain's history or state is treated.

Therefore, the security of a blockchain should be evaluated through its technical design, consensus rules, participant distribution and operational assumptions.

09. PUBLIC, PRIVATE AND PERMISSIONED NETWORKS

Blockchain networks can be designed with different access and participation models. These models influence who can read data, submit transactions and participate in validating or ordering records.

Public networks generally allow broad participation. Depending on the protocol, users may be able to read the ledger and submit transactions without receiving individual approval from a central operator.

Private networks restrict access to a defined group of participants. They may be operated within an organisation or among selected entities.

Permissioned networks require authorisation for certain activities, such as joining the network or validating transactions. They can be public or private in terms of data visibility, depending on their design.

These categories are not always mutually exclusive. A network can combine different access permissions for reading, writing and validation.

The choice of model affects governance, transparency, privacy, operational control and the assumptions participants must make about one another.

RESEARCH NOTE

Blockchain technology combines distributed data structures, cryptographic verification and network coordination.

Its significance lies not in a single technical feature, but in the interaction between these components. Blocks organise records, cryptographic hashes link data, digital signatures support authorisation, and network participants verify information according to protocol rules.

Understanding these foundations is essential before examining more advanced topics such as consensus mechanisms, smart contracts, scaling solutions and cross-chain systems.

Crypto Atlas approaches blockchain as a technological and institutional system whose capabilities and limitations must be examined through verifiable evidence.
