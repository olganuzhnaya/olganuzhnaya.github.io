Bitcoin Economics

The monetary architecture of Bitcoin: issuance, scarcity, mining incentives, transaction fees and long-term security.

1. Bitcoin's Monetary Model

Bitcoin has a monetary issuance schedule defined by the protocol. New bitcoin enter circulation through mining, while the amount that can be created in each block follows predetermined rules.

Unlike a conventional monetary system managed by a central bank, Bitcoin does not have a central institution responsible for adjusting its issuance schedule in response to economic conditions.

The rules governing issuance are implemented in Bitcoin software and independently checked by full nodes.

This design establishes a predictable monetary issuance process. It does not, by itself, determine the market price or purchasing power of bitcoin.

2. Bitcoin Supply and Issuance

Bitcoin's supply increases gradually as miners produce valid blocks and receive the permitted block subsidy.

At launch in January 2009, the initial block subsidy was 50 BTC. The subsidy is reduced by half after every 210,000 blocks.

Because the network targets an average block interval of approximately ten minutes, 210,000 blocks correspond to roughly four years. The actual calendar interval can vary.

The issuance schedule is therefore determined by block height rather than by a fixed calendar date.

The supply limit commonly described as 21 million bitcoin is a consequence of the repeated reduction in the block subsidy.

3. Block Subsidy and Mining Rewards

When a miner successfully produces a valid block, the block may contain a special coinbase transaction that claims the permitted block subsidy and transaction fees.

The block subsidy is the amount of newly created bitcoin that a miner is allowed to claim under the rules applicable to that block height.

Transaction fees are separate. They are the difference between the total value of transaction inputs and outputs, and they are collected from the transactions included in the block.

The miner's total block reward can therefore be described as:

Block Reward = Block Subsidy + Transaction Fees

A miner cannot simply choose to claim more bitcoin than the consensus rules permit. Full nodes independently validate the block and reject an excessive coinbase reward.

4. Bitcoin Halving

A halving is an automatic reduction of the block subsidy by 50 percent. It occurs every 210,000 blocks.

The subsidy has changed through the following historical stages:

  • 2009: 50 BTC per block.
  • 2012: 25 BTC per block.
  • 2016: 12.5 BTC per block.
  • 2020: 6.25 BTC per block.
  • April 2024: 3.125 BTC per block.

The next halving is expected around 2028, at block height 1,050,000. The subsidy will then become 1.5625 BTC per block.

These calendar dates are estimates. The protocol specifies block heights, not exact dates.

Halving reduces the rate at which new bitcoin enter circulation. It does not directly set the market price of bitcoin.

5. The 21 Million Supply Limit

Bitcoin is commonly described as having a maximum supply of 21 million BTC. This figure follows from the initial subsidy and the repeated halving schedule.

Each subsidy era lasts 210,000 blocks. The first era begins with 50 BTC per block, and each following era receives half the subsidy of the previous one.

The resulting issuance schedule forms a geometric series:

210,000 × 50 × (1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + ...) = 21,000,000 BTC

In actual Bitcoin implementation, amounts are represented in whole satoshis. Fractional satoshis are discarded when the subsidy is divided, so the theoretical total issued is slightly below 21 million BTC.

Full nodes enforce the permitted subsidy through block validation. A block that claims more than the allowed amount is invalid under those consensus rules.

The commonly quoted supply limit describes the issuance schedule. It should not be confused with the amount of bitcoin currently available for trading or the amount that individual holders can access.

6. Transaction Fees

Bitcoin transaction fees compensate miners for including transactions in blocks. They are paid by transaction creators through the difference between inputs and outputs.

Fees are commonly expressed as satoshis per virtual byte (sat/vB). The total fee depends on the transaction's virtual size and the fee rate selected.

Block space is limited. When demand for transaction inclusion increases, users may compete by offering higher fee rates.

Miners can consider the fees offered by transactions when selecting transactions for a block, subject to the applicable consensus and policy rules.

A higher fee rate can make a transaction more attractive for inclusion, but it does not guarantee confirmation at a particular time.

7. Mining Economics and Incentives

Bitcoin mining involves economic decisions as well as technical work. Miners commit computing resources and incur operating costs in an attempt to produce valid blocks.

Relevant costs can include electricity, mining hardware, cooling, facilities, maintenance, personnel and financing.

Mining revenue depends on the block subsidy, transaction fees, the miner's share of network computing power and the method used to participate in mining.

Solo miners receive the proceeds of a successfully mined block but face greater variability in the timing of rewards. Mining pools combine computing resources and distribute proceeds according to their payout arrangements.

Mining profitability is not guaranteed. Changes in bitcoin's market price, network difficulty, electricity costs, hardware efficiency and transaction fees can influence the economic outcome.

8. The Long-Term Security Model

Mining helps secure Bitcoin by making the production of blocks require computational work. This makes it costly to rewrite transaction history.

The block subsidy currently provides a significant part of the incentive available to miners. Because it decreases over time, transaction fees are expected to become increasingly important as a source of miner revenue.

Once the subsidy eventually reaches zero, miners will rely on transaction fees rather than newly issued bitcoin for block revenue.

This future transition raises important economic questions about block-space demand, fee-market behaviour, mining costs and the security incentives of the network.

The long-term outcome cannot be determined from the issuance schedule alone. It will also depend on future network usage, market conditions and the economic behaviour of participants.

Research Note

Bitcoin's monetary architecture combines a predetermined issuance schedule, a declining block subsidy and a fee-based incentive for transaction processing.

The relationship between issuance, mining costs, transaction demand and network security is an important subject of ongoing economic research.

A predictable supply schedule does not guarantee a particular market valuation or investment outcome.
