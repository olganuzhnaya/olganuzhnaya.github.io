PART II — THE ARCHITECTURE OF DIGITAL CAPITAL

Chapter 3. Tokenisation: Connecting Assets to Programmable Networks

How digital representations of assets can connect ownership records, money and financial transactions — and why technology alone cannot guarantee legal ownership or financial stability.

← Chapter 1: The Internet of Capital Chapter 2: The Architecture of Digital Capital Chapter 3: Tokenisation

Tokenisation is often presented as a way to make almost any asset digital, transferable and programmable. That description captures part of the idea, but it can also hide the most important questions. What does a token actually represent? Which record determines who has the relevant rights? How does a transfer become legally effective? And what happens when a transaction fails, an intermediary becomes insolvent or a system must be recovered after an operational incident?

Tokenisation can connect asset records, transaction rules and forms of digital money in new ways. Its significance therefore depends not only on the technology used to create and transfer tokens, but also on the legal arrangements, settlement assets, governance and institutions surrounding them.

1. What Does Tokenisation Mean?

Tokenisation is the process of representing rights to an asset, a financial claim or another form of value in a digital token recorded and managed through a defined technical system. Depending on the legal and operational design, the token may represent an existing right, form part of the authoritative record of that right, or create a claim against an issuer or intermediary.

These arrangements are not equivalent. A token that records a contractual claim against an issuer does not automatically give its holder direct ownership of an underlying building, bond or other asset. The legal effect depends on the governing law, the contractual documentation, the custody structure and the rules that determine which records are authoritative.

Tokenisation should therefore be understood as a combination of at least three elements:

  • Representation: what the token is intended to represent — for example, a security, a deposit claim, a fund interest or a right to receive an asset.
  • Record and transfer: how the token is created, held, transferred and reconciled with other records.
  • Legal and institutional framework: which rights are recognised, who is responsible for the system, and how disputes, insolvency and errors are handled.

A token can be technically transferable without every legal consequence of that transfer being settled. Technical capability and legal effect must be evaluated separately, then examined together.

2. What Can Be Tokenised?

Tokenisation can be applied to different kinds of assets and claims, but the implementation and legal consequences vary significantly.

  • Financial instruments: shares, bonds, fund units and other securities may be issued or represented in tokenised form under applicable securities and market-infrastructure rules.
  • Money and payment claims: tokenised deposits, certain forms of electronic money and stablecoins can provide different ways to represent monetary value. They differ in issuer, legal claim, redemption arrangements and risk.
  • Physical assets: a token may be linked to a claim concerning property, commodities or other tangible assets. The link must be legally and operationally enforceable; the token alone does not prove that the underlying asset is securely held or that ownership has transferred.
  • Rights and records: licences, entitlements, membership rights or other claims may be represented digitally where the applicable legal framework permits it.

The key question is not simply whether an asset can be represented by a token. It is whether the token's rights, transfer rules and relationship to the underlying asset are clear enough for the intended use.

3. Why Programmability Matters

A programmable token can be designed to follow rules encoded in software. Those rules may help automate eligibility checks, payment conditions, corporate actions, distribution of proceeds or other parts of a transaction. A system may also coordinate several steps that would otherwise be processed across separate databases and intermediaries.

For example, a transaction could be designed so that a transfer of a financial instrument is linked to a corresponding payment. A system might also apply predefined restrictions, such as allowing transfers only to eligible participants. Such automation can reduce manual processing and make transaction conditions easier to apply consistently.

However, software can enforce only the conditions it has been designed and authorised to enforce. It cannot independently determine whether a contract is valid under the relevant law, whether a real-world asset exists, whether information supplied to the system is accurate or whether a court will recognise a particular claim. External facts may require trusted data providers, legal procedures or institutional oversight.

Programmability is therefore not a substitute for sound legal drafting, governance, controls or accountability. It is a way to implement defined rules within a system.

4. Delivery versus Payment: Linking Asset Transfers and Money

One important use of coordinated financial infrastructure is delivery versus payment (DvP). Delivery versus payment is a securities settlement mechanism that links the securities transfer and the corresponding funds transfer so that final delivery occurs if and only if final payment occurs.

The purpose is to reduce the risk that one party delivers securities but does not receive the corresponding payment, or pays without receiving the securities. When DvP is legally and operationally effective, it eliminates principal risk in the exchange. It does not eliminate liquidity risk, replacement-cost risk, operational risk or credit risk associated with the settlement asset.

DvP is not unique to tokenisation. It can be implemented in conventional financial-market infrastructure as well as in systems that use tokenised assets or money. Nor does DvP necessarily require both transfers to occur simultaneously within one technical transaction. Atomic settlement — where linked transfers either complete together or do not complete — is one possible implementation, not the only one.

Tokenised infrastructure may make it easier to coordinate asset and payment legs, but a technical link is not enough by itself. The legal basis for finality of both transfers must be clear, including how the arrangement operates in insolvency. The system also needs procedures for failed transactions, error correction, disputes and operational recovery.

The Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures and the International Organization of Securities Commissions address settlement, finality and related risks in the Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures. Their framework remains useful when evaluating new technical arrangements: changing the technology does not remove the need to manage legal, credit, liquidity and operational risks.

5. Potential Benefits — and Their Limits

Depending on the design and the environment in which it operates, tokenisation may offer several potential benefits:

  • More integrated processing: asset records and transaction workflows may be coordinated on shared or interoperable infrastructure.
  • Programmable transaction conditions: some rules can be applied automatically, reducing the need for repeated manual instructions.
  • New settlement arrangements: tokenised assets may be linked to suitable forms of digital money, potentially improving coordination between delivery and payment.
  • Greater traceability: authorised participants may be able to inspect a shared transaction record, subject to privacy, confidentiality and access controls.
  • New product and service designs: tokenisation may support fractionalised interests or new ways to distribute and administer financial products where law and market demand permit them.

These are possible outcomes, not automatic results. A tokenised process may reproduce existing inefficiencies in a new technical environment. Benefits depend on the quality of the design, adoption by relevant participants, integration with existing systems, governance and the legal framework.

Financial stability considerations

Tokenisation can also introduce or amplify risks. These may include liquidity and maturity mismatches, leverage, uncertainty about the quality or value of assets, interconnectedness between institutions and platforms, concentration of service providers, and operational or cyber vulnerabilities. If systems are fragmented or depend on complex chains of intermediaries, stress can be difficult to observe and manage.

In its October 2024 assessment, the Financial Stability Board reported that adoption of DLT-based tokenisation of financial assets — including tokenised money potentially used for settlement — was then very low but growing and did not pose a material financial-stability risk at that time because of its limited scale. The assessment identified vulnerabilities that could become more relevant if tokenisation expanded, if complexity or opacity produced unpredictable outcomes under stress, or if risks were not adequately addressed. This is a dated assessment of the situation examined in 2024, not a claim about the scale of tokenisation in every later period.

Tokenisation should consequently be assessed as financial infrastructure, not only as a software innovation. The relevant questions include how risks are distributed, who can access liquidity during stress, whether participants can exit or recover assets, and how critical services continue during disruption.

6. The Legal Question: What Does the Token Represent?

The central legal question is the relationship between the token and the right it is intended to represent. Several structures are possible.

  • The token may itself form part of the legally recognised instrument or authoritative record of ownership.
  • The token may represent a right recorded in another system, with the legal effect of a transfer depending on that system and the governing rules.
  • The token may give its holder a contractual claim against an issuer or intermediary, without giving the holder a direct proprietary interest in the underlying asset.

These structures should not be treated as interchangeable. An enforceable contractual claim does not necessarily amount to ownership of the underlying asset. Depending on the arrangement, a token holder may be an unsecured creditor of an issuer or intermediary rather than the owner of a segregated asset.

Evaluation should therefore consider custody arrangements, segregation of client assets, insolvency priority, the authoritative ownership record, transfer restrictions, redemption rights and the procedures available if the issuer or service provider fails. The answer depends on the specific instrument and jurisdiction; a general label such as “tokenised asset” cannot settle these questions.

This does not mean that tokenised assets are inherently legally incomplete. Under an appropriate legal framework, a token may constitute or represent a legally recognised instrument or right. The point is that legal status must be established by the relevant rules and documentation, rather than inferred from the technical existence of a token.

7. From Individual Tokens to Connected Financial Infrastructure

The broader significance of tokenisation may emerge when tokenised assets, payment instruments and financial institutions can interact through connected infrastructure. If asset records and settlement money are represented in compatible forms, transaction steps that are now separated across systems may become easier to coordinate.

The Bank for International Settlements' 2025 Annual Economic Report discusses a vision of a unified ledger: a common programmable platform that could bring together tokenised central bank reserves, commercial bank money and financial assets, with government bonds used as a central example. The concept is a possible direction for the monetary and financial system, not an assertion that a single global ledger already exists. A unified ledger also need not necessarily use distributed ledger technology; the institutional and functional design matters more than a technology label.

Such a system would still require clear rules for access, privacy, governance, settlement finality, resilience and the roles of public and private institutions. It would also need to preserve the distinction between different forms of money. Central bank money, commercial bank deposits and privately issued stablecoins are not identical claims and do not carry the same issuer, legal or risk characteristics.

Interoperability is not achieved simply by connecting software interfaces. Systems must also align, or clearly bridge, their legal rules, identities, data standards, risk controls and procedures for handling failure. Without this work, technical connectivity can coexist with legal and operational fragmentation.

8. A Practical Framework for Evaluating Tokenisation

A careful assessment of a tokenised product or system can begin with the following questions:

  1. What is the token? Identify the precise asset, instrument or claim it represents.
  2. Which law applies? Determine the relevant jurisdiction, governing documents and legal basis for the holder's rights.
  3. Which record is authoritative? Establish whether the token record itself determines ownership or must be reconciled with another register or legal record.
  4. How are rights transferred? Identify the conditions for a valid transfer, settlement finality and any restrictions on eligible holders.
  5. What money is used for settlement? Assess the issuer, redemption mechanism, credit exposure and legal characteristics of the settlement asset.
  6. What happens on insolvency? Examine custody, segregation, creditor priority, recovery rights and the treatment of client assets.
  7. How are failures handled? Review cybersecurity, operational resilience, error correction, disputes, reversals where legally possible and recovery procedures.
  8. Who governs the system? Identify the operator, administrators, data providers, decision-making rules and accountability mechanisms.
  9. Where do risks accumulate? Consider liquidity, leverage, concentration, interconnections and dependencies on third-party providers.
  10. What is actually improved? Compare the proposed arrangement with the existing process and look for measurable benefits rather than assuming that tokenisation itself guarantees efficiency.

This framework helps separate technological claims from legal and economic realities. It also makes comparisons between different tokenisation projects more meaningful.

Conclusion

Tokenisation can connect digital representations of assets with programmable transaction rules and forms of digital money. It may improve coordination, automate defined processes and support new financial-market arrangements. Yet none of these possibilities is guaranteed by the token itself.

The decisive questions concern what rights the token represents, how transfers become legally effective, what asset settles the payment, who is accountable for the infrastructure and how risks are controlled. Delivery versus payment illustrates the point: coordinated technology can support safer settlement, but legal finality, liquidity and operational resilience remain essential.

The long-term importance of tokenisation will depend not simply on how many assets become digital tokens, but on whether the resulting systems provide reliable rights, effective settlement and trustworthy financial infrastructure.
