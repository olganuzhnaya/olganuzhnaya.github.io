1. Beyond the Blockchain

The development of digital finance is often discussed primarily in terms of blockchain technology. Distributed ledgers, smart contracts and digital tokens have become central to conversations about the future of money and financial markets.

Yet the architecture of digital capital extends beyond any individual technology.

A financial system must do more than record transactions. It must represent economic value, establish the rights associated with assets, enable transfers, provide mechanisms for settlement and determine how those rights are recognised and enforced.

These functions are related, but they are not identical.

A blockchain can record that a token has moved from one address to another. It cannot, by that technical operation alone, establish every legal consequence of the transfer. A token may represent a financial claim without being the underlying asset itself. A transaction may be confirmed on a network while questions about legal finality, custody or the enforceability of rights remain dependent on other arrangements.

Understanding digital capital therefore requires a broader analytical perspective.

The central question is not simply how assets can be placed on a blockchain. It is how digital representations, monetary instruments, financial infrastructure, institutions and legal systems can interact to support reliable economic activity.

This chapter develops a framework for examining those relationships. It distinguishes the technological mechanisms used to record and transfer value from the economic rights, settlement arrangements and legal structures that give those operations their significance.

The objective is to understand the emerging architecture of digital capital without assuming that every system will use the same technology, follow the same institutional model or evolve at the same pace.

2. The Layers of Digital Capital

Digital capital can be examined through six interconnected analytical layers.

These layers are not necessarily separate technical components. They are a framework for identifying the different functions that must be considered when evaluating a digital financial system.

Layer 1. Asset and Economic Value

The first layer concerns the economic substance of what is represented.

An asset may be a financial security, a bank deposit, a commodity, real estate, a contractual claim or a digital-native asset. Each category has different characteristics, risks and legal implications.

Before evaluating the technology used to represent an asset, it is necessary to establish what the asset actually is, who has rights in it and which obligations those rights create.

A digital representation does not eliminate the economic characteristics of the underlying asset.

Layer 2. Digital Representation and Ownership Records

The second layer concerns how assets and claims are represented digitally.

A token may identify an asset, represent a contractual claim or function as a transferable instrument within a particular system. The relationship between the token and the underlying asset depends on the system's design and applicable law.

The digital record may support identification, transfer and verification. However, the legal meaning of that record cannot automatically be assumed.

Layer 3. Money and Settlement

The third layer concerns the monetary instruments used to make payments and settle obligations.

Possible settlement instruments include central bank money, commercial bank money and privately issued digital instruments such as stablecoins.

These instruments differ in issuer, redemption arrangements, counterparty exposure and regulatory treatment.

The distinction matters because the transfer of an asset and the settlement of the payment for that asset are related but separate operations.

Layer 4. Networks and Interoperability

The fourth layer concerns the infrastructure through which information, assets and payments move.

Different systems may use public blockchains, permissioned distributed ledgers, conventional databases or combinations of these technologies.

Their ability to exchange information does not necessarily mean that they can transfer assets, coordinate settlement or recognise one another's legal records.

Interoperability must therefore be evaluated at both the technical and institutional levels.

Layer 5. Institutions, Governance and Risk Management

The fifth layer concerns the organisations and rules that support the system.

Financial institutions, central banks, custodians, market infrastructures, technology providers and regulators may perform different functions.

Governance arrangements determine who can operate the infrastructure, change its rules, resolve disputes and respond to operational failures.

A technically efficient system can still be unreliable if its responsibilities, incentives and recovery mechanisms are poorly designed.

Layer 6. Legal Recognition and Enforcement

The sixth layer concerns the legal status of assets, claims and transactions.

Legal systems determine how rights are recognised, transferred, protected and enforced. They also influence the treatment of assets in insolvency, disputes involving intermediaries and conflicts between jurisdictions.

Technical control, economic entitlement and legal ownership may coincide, but they should not be treated as interchangeable concepts.

A Framework of Interdependence

The six layers form an analytical framework rather than a universal blueprint.

A system may combine several layers within a single platform or distribute them among multiple institutions and networks. Some functions may be automated, while others remain dependent on conventional legal and operational arrangements.

The framework is useful because it prevents a common analytical mistake: treating technological functionality as proof that every economic, institutional and legal requirement has been resolved.

The following sections examine these relationships in greater detail, beginning with the distinction between money, assets and the transactions that connect them.

3. What Exactly Moves Through a Digital Financial Network?

A digital financial transaction may involve several different objects: an asset, a token representing a claim, a payment instrument and a record of the resulting transfer.

These objects may be connected, but they do not necessarily have the same legal or economic meaning.

3.1 Money Is Not the Same as an Asset

Money is commonly used as a medium of exchange, a unit of account and a means of settling obligations.

Assets, by contrast, may be held for investment, generate income, provide access to services or represent contractual rights.

A financial security may be transferred in exchange for money. A tokenised deposit may be used to make a payment. A stablecoin may be transferred as consideration for another digital asset.

In each case, it is important to distinguish the asset being transferred from the instrument used to pay for it.

The distinction becomes particularly important when the two operations occur on different systems or under different legal arrangements.

3.2 A Digital Token Is Not Necessarily the Underlying Asset

A token may represent a claim against an issuer, an interest in a fund, a contractual right or another form of economic exposure.

Its relationship with the underlying asset depends on the legal and technical structure.

For example, a token representing an interest in a financial instrument may be associated with an underlying register, a custodian or an issuer's contractual obligations. The token may provide evidence of a right, form part of the mechanism for transferring that right or operate under a legal framework that gives the digital record a more direct role.

These arrangements are not equivalent.

If the token and the underlying asset are not legally linked in an enforceable manner, transferring the token may not produce the intended transfer of rights in the underlying asset.

The relevant question is therefore not merely whether a token exists, but what rights it represents and how those rights are recognised.

3.3 Settlement: When Does a Transaction Become Final?

Settlement is the process through which the obligations arising from a transaction are discharged.

In a securities transaction, one party may need to deliver the security while the other delivers the payment. Delivery versus payment, or DvP, is an arrangement designed to link these two obligations so that delivery and payment occur together under the relevant settlement mechanism.

A distributed ledger may coordinate these operations and provide a shared record of their execution.

However, technical execution and legal finality are distinct questions.

Technical execution concerns whether the system has processed the transaction according to its rules. Legal finality concerns whether the transfer and discharge of obligations are legally effective and protected against relevant forms of reversal or challenge.

The applicable legal framework, settlement arrangements and system rules determine how these questions are answered.

3.4 Why This Matters for the Internet of Capital

The emerging digital financial system may connect assets, payment instruments and settlement mechanisms across multiple networks.

To evaluate such a system, it is necessary to identify what moves, what obligation is discharged, which institution or protocol performs each function and what legal consequences follow.

Without these distinctions, technical demonstrations can be mistaken for complete financial solutions.

A successful transfer of a token is an important technical event. Whether it also produces the intended economic and legal outcome depends on the broader architecture.

4. The Relationship Between Tokens, Money and Trust

Every financial system relies on trust, although the location and distribution of that trust differ.

In conventional banking, participants depend on banks, payment systems, central banks, legal arrangements and supervisory institutions. A payment may be supported by account records, contractual obligations and established settlement procedures.

In a public blockchain system such as Bitcoin, transaction validation and the ordering of transactions depend on a different combination of cryptographic mechanisms, network rules and economic incentives.

Smart-contract platforms can extend these capabilities by allowing predefined operations to be executed according to code. Yet the operation of a smart contract does not remove every need for trust. Users may still depend on software quality, network security, external data providers, governance arrangements, custody services and applicable law.

Trust is therefore not simply eliminated by digitisation. It may be redistributed among different mechanisms and participants.

4.1 Different Models of Trust

Bitcoin illustrates a model in which transaction verification and the maintenance of a shared ledger are organised through a public network governed by protocol rules.

Bank-based payment systems operate through institutional relationships, account structures and settlement arrangements.

Hybrid systems may combine distributed ledgers with regulated institutions, conventional money and established legal frameworks.

Each model has different trade-offs involving openness, control, scalability, privacy, governance and legal accountability.

No single model should be assumed to solve every financial use case.

4.2 The Importance of the Monetary Instrument

A tokenised financial asset may be transferred on a distributed ledger while its payment leg relies on a separate system.

The settlement instrument influences the overall risk profile. Relevant considerations include the issuer's creditworthiness, redemption rights, liquidity, legal status and the conditions under which payment is considered final.

Central bank money, commercial bank money and stablecoins are not interchangeable merely because each can be represented or transferred digitally.

Their differences remain relevant even when the surrounding technology is similar.

4.3 The Crypto Atlas Research Perspective

The relationship between tokens, money and trust should be examined through three questions:

  • Representation: What economic value or claim does the digital instrument represent?
  • Settlement: Which instrument discharges the payment obligation, and under what conditions?
  • Accountability: Who is responsible when the system fails, a claim is disputed or a transaction produces an unintended result?

These questions help distinguish the capabilities of digital technology from the institutional arrangements required for dependable financial activity.

5. From Separate Systems to Connected Financial Infrastructure

Financial markets have historically developed through multiple specialised systems.

Payment networks, securities depositories, trading venues, custodians and banking infrastructures perform different functions. They exchange information and coordinate operations, but their technical standards, operating rules and settlement procedures may differ.

Tokenisation creates opportunities to reconsider how these functions interact.

A digital asset may be issued, transferred and serviced through a programmable platform. Payment instructions and asset records may be coordinated within a shared environment. Some reconciliation and processing tasks may become more automated.

The potential benefit is not simply a faster record of ownership. It is the possibility of reducing friction between processes that are currently distributed across institutions and systems.

5.1 The Interoperability Challenge

Interoperability is the ability of different systems to work together in a meaningful way.

At the technical level, systems may exchange messages or recognise common data formats.

At the operational level, they must coordinate processes, permissions and transaction states.

At the financial level, they must support the movement or settlement of relevant assets and obligations.

At the legal level, participants must understand what the records and actions mean under the applicable rules.

These levels are connected but distinct.

Two platforms may exchange information without being able to transfer an asset between them. They may transfer a token without ensuring that the underlying legal right changes hands. They may coordinate technical execution without sharing a common settlement framework.

Meaningful interoperability therefore requires more than a technical connection.

5.2 Atomic Settlement and Delivery versus Payment

Atomic settlement refers to an arrangement in which linked operations are executed together or do not complete as intended under the relevant system rules.

In a delivery-versus-payment arrangement, the transfer of an asset is linked to the corresponding payment.

Such mechanisms can reduce the risk that one party delivers an asset without receiving the expected payment, provided the arrangement is implemented effectively and supported by appropriate legal and operational safeguards.

Smart contracts and programmable settlement mechanisms may help coordinate these operations.

Nevertheless, atomic execution does not automatically resolve every risk. Questions may remain about the legal status of the assets, the availability of the settlement instrument, operational failures, external dependencies and the enforceability of the transaction.

The broader objective is to align technical coordination with reliable economic and legal outcomes.

6. The Role of Money in a Tokenised Financial System

The design of a tokenised financial system depends not only on how assets are represented, but also on the form of money used to settle transactions.

Different monetary instruments create different relationships among users, issuers, financial institutions and central banks.

Three categories are particularly relevant to current discussions.

6.1 Central Bank Money

Central bank money is a liability of a central bank. In conventional financial markets, it plays an important role in the settlement of obligations between eligible institutions.

The possibility of using central bank money in new digital settlement arrangements has become an important area of institutional experimentation.

A tokenised or otherwise digitally integrated representation of central bank money could potentially support the settlement of transactions involving tokenised assets while retaining the characteristics associated with central bank liabilities.

The exact design would depend on the central bank's framework, eligibility requirements, technical architecture and applicable law.

Such proposals should not be confused with a claim that a single, universally available tokenised central bank settlement system already exists.

6.2 Tokenised Commercial Bank Money

Commercial bank money consists primarily of claims that customers hold against commercial banks, such as bank deposits.

Tokenised commercial bank money seeks to represent or transfer these claims through digital infrastructure.

Its significance lies in the possibility of integrating existing banking relationships with programmable transaction environments.

However, the legal and economic nature of the claim remains important. A token representing a bank deposit does not cease to depend on the issuing bank merely because its transfer is processed through a distributed ledger.

Questions of redemption, access, settlement, bank credit exposure and regulatory treatment continue to matter.

6.3 Stablecoins

Stablecoins are digital tokens designed to maintain a relatively stable value, often by reference to a currency such as the US dollar.

Their structures vary. Some rely on reserve assets and redemption arrangements; others use different mechanisms to seek price stability.

Stablecoins can support digital asset trading and transfers across certain networks. Their practical usefulness, however, depends on factors such as liquidity, market access, redemption rights, reserve quality, governance and the applicable regulatory framework.

A stablecoin should not automatically be treated as equivalent to central bank money or a commercial bank deposit.

The issuer, legal claim, reserve structure and redemption mechanism determine important aspects of its risk profile.

6.4 Why the Choice of Settlement Asset Matters

The settlement instrument influences counterparty risk, liquidity management and the conditions under which a transaction can be completed.

A technically integrated platform may support several settlement instruments, each with different characteristics.

The appropriate choice depends on the transaction, the participants, the relevant legal framework and the risks that the system is designed to manage.

The Bank for International Settlements has discussed the potential of connecting tokenised central bank reserves, commercial bank money and other financial assets within a more integrated monetary and financial architecture. This is an important direction of institutional analysis, not proof that one model will be adopted universally.

The central design question is how digital infrastructure can improve the coordination of assets and payments while maintaining sound settlement arrangements and clear accountability.

7. From Institutional Experiments to Financial Infrastructure

Institutional experiments provide evidence about whether proposed digital financial arrangements can operate under defined conditions.

They can test transaction processing, settlement mechanisms, technical interoperability and coordination among participating organisations.

One important example is the Eurosystem's exploratory work on new technologies for wholesale central bank money settlement.

According to the European Central Bank, the initiative conducted between May and November 2024 involved 64 eligible participants across nine jurisdictions. Across the trials and experiments, nearly €1.6 billion in central bank money was settled.

These results demonstrate that institutional participants were able to test and execute transactions using the arrangements developed for the initiative. They provide useful evidence about practical implementation.

They do not, by themselves, establish that a single architecture has been selected for all future financial markets or that the tested arrangements are ready for universal adoption.

A subsequent development marked a further step towards implementation. On 21 September 2026, the European Central Bank announced the launch of Pontes, the Eurosystem's solution for settling transactions in tokenised assets using central bank money. The initiative is intended to support the gradual integration of banks and market infrastructures into tokenised financial markets. Its launch demonstrates progress from experimentation towards operational infrastructure, while broader adoption and the development of a more integrated ecosystem remain ongoing processes.

7.1 What Experiments Can Establish

A well-designed experiment can provide evidence about:

  • Whether a technical mechanism performs its intended function.
  • How different systems coordinate transaction processing and settlement.
  • Which operational and legal issues arise in practice.
  • What adjustments may be needed before broader deployment.
  • How institutions can cooperate within a defined experimental framework.

The value of an experiment lies in both successful outcomes and the problems it reveals.

7.2 The Problem of Scaling

Moving from an experiment to production infrastructure introduces additional requirements.

A system that works for a limited group of participants may face different demands when it must support larger transaction volumes, multiple jurisdictions, diverse assets and more complex institutional relationships.

Scaling also requires attention to resilience, cybersecurity, liquidity, governance, operational continuity and recovery procedures.

Legal questions may become more complex when transactions cross jurisdictions or involve different types of intermediaries.

Financial stability considerations may also change as interconnectedness and exposure increase. The Financial Stability Board has highlighted potential vulnerabilities associated with tokenisation, including liquidity and maturity mismatches, leverage, interconnectedness and operational fragilities if these activities expand.

The significance of these vulnerabilities depends on the design, scale and interconnectedness of the arrangements involved.

The appropriate conclusion is neither that tokenisation is inherently unsafe nor that technical success guarantees financial stability.

It is that wider adoption requires evidence, governance and risk management proportionate to the system's role.

8. The Emerging Architecture: Integration Without Assuming Uniformity

The future of digital capital is unlikely to be determined by a single technology alone.

Different financial activities have different requirements. Public networks may be appropriate for some applications, while regulated wholesale settlement, securities infrastructure or institutional payment arrangements may require other governance and access models.

Some systems may use shared programmable platforms. Others may connect specialised networks through common standards and coordination mechanisms. Hybrid architectures may combine conventional databases, distributed ledgers and established financial institutions.

These approaches are not necessarily mutually exclusive.

8.1 Shared Infrastructure and Specialised Networks

A shared platform may simplify coordination by allowing multiple functions to operate within a common environment.

However, shared infrastructure can also concentrate operational dependencies and create common points of failure.

Specialised networks may offer greater control over particular functions, but they can introduce additional integration and reconciliation requirements.

The design challenge is to determine which functions benefit from integration and which require separation, independent oversight or specialised controls.

8.2 Integration Does Not Mean Uniformity

An interconnected digital financial system does not need to rely on identical technologies or identical legal structures.

It does, however, require sufficiently clear rules for communicating information, transferring assets, settling obligations and resolving inconsistencies.

The goal is functional compatibility rather than technological uniformity.

8.3 Three Distinctions for Evaluation

The Crypto Atlas framework emphasises three distinctions.

First, technical connectivity is not the same as interoperability. Systems may exchange data without supporting the transfer of legally recognised rights or the coordinated settlement of obligations.

Second, settlement does not eliminate every form of risk. The design of the settlement mechanism matters, but so do legal finality, operational resilience, counterparty exposure and recovery arrangements.

Third, a successful experiment is not universal adoption. Evidence from trials can inform future decisions, but production deployment requires additional technical, economic, legal and institutional validation.

These distinctions provide a basis for evaluating emerging infrastructure without overstating what current implementations demonstrate.

The architecture of digital capital must ultimately support not only the movement of value, but also the rights and responsibilities associated with that movement. This leads to the next question: what does it mean to own and control a digital asset?

9. Ownership, Control and Legal Recognition

Digital ownership is often discussed as if control of a private key were equivalent to ownership of an asset.

That assumption is too broad.

A private key may provide the technical ability to authorise transactions from a particular blockchain address. It does not necessarily establish that the person controlling the key has the strongest legal claim to the associated asset.

Similarly, a person may have a legally recognised entitlement without directly controlling the relevant digital infrastructure.

To understand digital ownership, three concepts must be distinguished:

  • Technical control: The practical ability to operate or transfer a digital asset using the relevant system.
  • Economic rights: The benefits, claims or obligations associated with the asset.
  • Legal ownership or entitlement: The rights recognised under the applicable legal framework.

These concepts may overlap, but their relationship depends on the asset, the custody arrangement, the governing law and the facts of the transaction.

9.1 The Private Key and the Limits of Control

A private key is a cryptographic credential used to authorise certain actions within a digital asset system.

Possession or control of the key may allow someone to transfer tokens, sign transactions or interact with smart contracts.

However, technical capability and legal entitlement are not identical.

A key may be controlled by a custodian acting for a client. It may be stolen or used without authorisation. It may be held by a person who has no legitimate claim to the associated asset.

The blockchain may record an authorised transaction according to its protocol rules even when the surrounding circumstances create a legal dispute.

The system's record is important evidence of what occurred on the network. It does not necessarily settle every question about the parties' rights.

9.2 Custody and the Separation of Rights

Digital assets may be held directly by an individual or through a custodian, exchange, broker or other intermediary.

Custody arrangements determine who controls the keys, how assets are recorded and what rights clients have against the intermediary.

The legal and operational structure matters.

For example, assets held under a custody arrangement may be treated differently from assets that form part of an intermediary's own estate. The result depends on the applicable law, contractual terms, segregation arrangements and the facts of the case.

A user interface that displays an asset balance does not, by itself, establish whether the user holds the asset directly, has a claim against an intermediary or has another form of entitlement.

Reliable digital ownership therefore requires clarity about the relationship among control, custody, economic rights and legal claims.

10. Tokenisation and the Legal Identity of an Asset

Tokenisation creates a digital representation of an asset, claim or other right within a particular technical environment.

Its economic significance depends on what is represented and what the token enables its holder to do.

Tokenisation may facilitate the recording and transfer of interests, automate certain processes and make some forms of asset administration more efficient.

However, the creation of a token and the legal transfer of an asset are not necessarily the same event.

10.1 Representing an Asset Is Not the Same as Transferring It

Suppose a token is created to represent an interest in a financial asset.

The token may be transferable between blockchain addresses. Yet the legal consequences of that transfer depend on the arrangement connecting the token to the underlying interest.

In one structure, the relevant legal framework may recognise the digital record as part of the mechanism by which rights are transferred.

In another, the token may represent a contractual claim against an issuer or intermediary, while a separate register or legal process determines ownership of the underlying asset.

In a third structure, the connection may depend on contractual obligations and operational procedures that must be followed outside the blockchain.

These differences can affect the rights of buyers, sellers, custodians and other participants.

The key question is whether the transfer of the token produces the intended transfer of rights under the applicable legal framework.

10.2 The Link Between the Token and the Underlying Asset

A tokenised asset arrangement should clearly establish:

  • What the token represents.
  • Who is responsible for issuing and maintaining the representation.
  • Which rights the holder receives.
  • How the token is linked to the underlying asset or claim.
  • What happens if the underlying asset changes, becomes unavailable or is subject to a dispute.
  • How rights can be enforced if the issuer or intermediary fails.

The link between the token and the underlying asset may be supported by legal documentation, a register, custody arrangements, contractual obligations or a combination of mechanisms.

Technology can help maintain records and automate processes, but it cannot make an unclear legal relationship reliable merely by recording it on a blockchain.

The stronger the connection between the digital representation and the enforceable rights it is intended to represent, the more meaningful the tokenisation arrangement becomes.

11. Legal Recognition Across Jurisdictions

Digital assets operate across networks that may be accessible from many countries, but legal rights do not automatically become uniform across national borders.

Different jurisdictions may classify assets differently, apply different rules to custody and transfer, and adopt different approaches to insolvency and enforcement.

This creates a fundamental challenge for systems designed to operate internationally.

11.1 An Evolving Legal Framework

Legal approaches to digital assets continue to develop.

In England and Wales, the Property (Digital Assets etc) Act 2025 received Royal Assent on 2 December 2025. The legislation clarifies the treatment of certain digital or electronic things as property under the law of England and Wales, without requiring them to fit exclusively within traditional categories of personal property.

The significance of this development should be understood within its jurisdictional scope. It does not create a single global legal definition of digital ownership or automatically harmonise the law of other countries.

At the international level, UNIDROIT's Principles on Digital Assets and Private Law, adopted in May 2023 and published in October 2023, address legal questions relating to digital assets and private law.

The Principles consider matters including control, transfers, custody, secured transactions, insolvency and private international law. They provide an international reference framework, but their practical effect depends on the relevant legal context and their implementation or use by jurisdictions and legal actors.

These developments illustrate a broader trend: legal systems are working to clarify how existing principles and new legal approaches apply to digital assets.

11.2 Why Jurisdiction Matters

A digital asset may be controlled by a person in one country, held through a service provider in another and connected to an issuer or underlying asset in a third.

If a dispute arises, several questions may need to be answered:

  • Which law governs the relevant rights?
  • Which court or authority has jurisdiction?
  • How should the asset be treated if an intermediary becomes insolvent?
  • Can a judgment or legal remedy be enforced where the relevant assets are located?
  • How should competing claims be resolved?

The answers may depend on the asset's structure, contractual arrangements, the parties involved and applicable conflict-of-laws rules.

A globally accessible network is not the same as a globally uniform legal system.

For the architecture of digital capital, legal interoperability is therefore an important consideration alongside technical interoperability.

12. Insolvency, Recovery and the Meaning of Ownership

The quality of an ownership structure becomes particularly clear when something goes wrong.

Under normal conditions, a user may be able to view a balance, authorise a transfer and interact with a digital asset without difficulty.

In a failure scenario, the same user may need to establish entitlement, recover access, challenge an unauthorised transfer or separate their assets from those of an insolvent intermediary.

These situations reveal whether the arrangement provides meaningful protection beyond technical functionality.

12.1 Technical Records and Legal Remedies

Digital records can provide evidence about transaction history, addresses, timestamps and the sequence of recorded operations.

Such evidence may be valuable when investigating a dispute.

However, a record of a transfer does not necessarily resolve whether the transfer was authorised, whether the recipient acquired an enforceable legal entitlement or whether a remedy is available.

Legal remedies may require cooperation from custodians, courts, administrators or other institutions. In some circumstances, recovery may be difficult or impossible even where the relevant transaction is clearly visible on a public blockchain.

The availability of technical evidence should therefore not be confused with the availability of an effective legal remedy.

12.2 Designing for Failure, Not Only for Success

Digital financial infrastructure should be evaluated under both normal and adverse conditions.

Relevant questions include:

  • What happens if a custodian becomes insolvent?
  • How are client assets identified and separated?
  • Can access be recovered if keys are lost or compromised?
  • What procedures apply if an intermediary stops operating?
  • How are unauthorised transfers investigated?
  • Who has authority to resolve disputes?
  • Can the relevant rights be enforced across jurisdictions?

The answers will vary by asset type and system design.

A decentralised protocol, a regulated custodian and a tokenised financial instrument may require different approaches to recovery and dispute resolution.

No single mechanism can guarantee recovery in every situation. Nevertheless, explicitly designing for failure helps identify weaknesses before they affect users and institutions.

For digital capital to support durable economic activity, the system must be evaluated not only by how efficiently it transfers value, but also by how it responds when ordinary assumptions no longer hold.

13. A Practical Framework for Evaluating Digital Ownership

The following framework provides a structured way to assess a digital asset or tokenisation arrangement.

It is intended as an analytical tool, not as a substitute for legal advice or a jurisdiction-specific legal assessment.

Question 1. What Exactly Is the Asset?

Identify the underlying economic object.

Is it a native digital asset, a financial security, a bank deposit, a contractual claim, a commodity or a representation of another asset?

The answer establishes the starting point for evaluating the arrangement.

Question 2. What Does the Token Represent?

Determine whether the token represents ownership, a beneficial interest, a contractual claim, an entitlement to redemption or another form of economic exposure.

The word token does not, by itself, identify the legal rights attached to an instrument.

Question 3. Who Controls the Asset Technically?

Establish who can authorise transactions and under what conditions.

Consider private-key arrangements, custodial relationships, access permissions and any mechanisms that may restrict or enable transfers.

Technical control should be evaluated separately from legal ownership.

Question 4. Which Rights Does the Holder Have?

Identify the rights associated with the asset or token.

These may include rights to transfer, redeem, receive income, exercise voting powers or make claims against an issuer or intermediary.

The relevant documentation and legal framework should explain how those rights arise and how they can be exercised.

Question 5. Which Law Governs the Arrangement?

Determine which jurisdiction's law governs the relevant rights, contractual relationships and transfers.

Where multiple jurisdictions are involved, assess whether the applicable rules are clear enough to support reliable transactions and dispute resolution.

Question 6. What Happens if a Participant Fails?

Consider insolvency, loss of access, unauthorised transactions, operational disruption and disputes over ownership.

Establish what recovery mechanisms exist and whether the holder's rights can be enforced if an issuer, custodian or intermediary fails.

Question 7. Can the Rights Be Enforced in Practice?

Finally, assess whether the claimed rights are supported by effective legal and operational mechanisms.

A right may be described in documentation but remain difficult to enforce if responsibilities are unclear, relevant records are inaccessible or remedies are unavailable in the appropriate jurisdiction.

The framework can be applied to different types of digital assets, including native cryptoassets, tokenised securities, tokenised deposits and stablecoins. The relevant considerations will differ according to the structure being examined.

The objective is to connect technical design with economic substance and enforceable rights.

14. The Economic Significance of Legally Recognised Digital Ownership

The long-term significance of digital capital depends on more than the ability to create and transfer tokens.

For digital representations to support a broad range of economic activities, participants need to understand what they represent, which rights they convey and how those rights are protected.

Clear ownership arrangements can support confidence among investors, financial institutions, custodians and other market participants. They can also help clarify responsibilities when transactions fail or competing claims arise.

Tokenisation may improve the administration and transfer of certain assets, enable programmable processes and support new forms of financial infrastructure. However, these potential benefits depend on the design of the system and the legal and institutional arrangements surrounding it.

The same technology can support very different outcomes depending on how rights, liabilities and responsibilities are structured.

Conclusion of This Section

The architecture of digital capital brings together several dimensions that must be evaluated together:

  • Digital representation determines how assets and claims are recorded.
  • Technical control determines who can perform particular actions within a system.
  • Economic rights determine the benefits and obligations associated with an asset.
  • Monetary and settlement arrangements determine how financial obligations are discharged.
  • Legal recognition determines how rights are characterised and protected.
  • Governance and recovery mechanisms determine how the system responds to disputes and failures.

These dimensions are connected, but none should be treated as a complete substitute for the others.

The Bank for International Settlements' work on tokenisation has highlighted the potential for programmable platforms to combine asset records, transfer mechanisms and rules within more integrated financial arrangements. At the same time, institutional and legal developments demonstrate that the wider implementation of such systems requires careful attention to settlement, governance, risk management and enforceability.

The emerging architecture of digital capital should therefore be understood as a process of institutional and technological development, rather than as the inevitable replacement of every existing financial structure by a single digital platform.

The central question is how to connect the advantages of digital infrastructure with the legal certainty, operational resilience and financial integrity required for lasting economic use.

Crypto Atlas Research Note

This chapter presents an analytical framework for examining digital capital across technological, economic, monetary, institutional and legal dimensions.

Its central proposition is that the significance of a digital asset cannot be established solely by examining the token or the network on which it operates.

A rigorous evaluation must distinguish technical control from economic entitlement, the representation of an asset from the rights attached to it, and technical transaction execution from legal settlement and enforceability.

The development of digital financial infrastructure should be assessed through both its potential efficiencies and the safeguards required for reliable operation.

The framework is intended to support further research into the evolving relationship between digital assets, financial markets, monetary systems and legal institutions.
