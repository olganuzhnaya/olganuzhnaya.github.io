What Is Ethereum?

A Beginner’s Guide to Programmable Blockchain

Understanding Ethereum, Ether, smart contracts, the EVM, gas, Proof-of-Stake and decentralized applications.

Education · Ethereum · Blockchain · Digital Assets

1. What Is Ethereum?

Ethereum is a blockchain network designed to support not only transfers of digital assets, but also programmable applications and smart contracts.

Ethereum's network maintains a shared state that is updated when transactions are processed and validated.

Unlike a traditional database controlled by one organization, Ethereum operates through a distributed network of computers called nodes.

2. Ethereum and Ether Are Not the Same Thing

Ethereum is the blockchain network and protocol.

Ether (ETH) is the native cryptocurrency of the Ethereum network.

The two terms are often used interchangeably in everyday conversation, but they describe different things.

When people talk about the market price of “Ethereum,” they are generally referring to the market price of ETH.

3. Why Was Ethereum Created?

Ethereum expanded the idea of blockchain-based systems toward general-purpose programmable computation.

Instead of using a blockchain primarily to record transfers of one native asset, Ethereum was designed so developers could deploy programs that operate according to predefined rules.

These programs are known as smart contracts.

4. What Is the Ethereum Virtual Machine?

The Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM) is the computational environment used by Ethereum to execute smart contract code.

The Ethereum network maintains agreement about the state of this virtual machine.

When a valid transaction requests computation, Ethereum nodes process that request according to the protocol's rules and update the network state.

5. What Are Smart Contracts?

A smart contract is a program that runs on a blockchain.

On Ethereum, smart contracts contain code and data and are deployed at blockchain addresses.

Users can interact with smart contracts by sending transactions that request specific functions to be executed.

Smart contracts can be used to create applications such as decentralized exchanges, lending protocols, games, token systems and other programmable services.

6. Are Smart Contracts Legal Contracts?

Not necessarily.

The term “smart contract” describes a program operating according to blockchain rules. It does not automatically mean that the program constitutes a legal contract under a particular jurisdiction.

The legal status of an application or agreement depends on the applicable law and the circumstances involved.

7. What Is ETH Used For?

ETH is the native asset of Ethereum.

One of its fundamental functions is paying transaction and computation fees on the network.

ETH is also used within Ethereum's Proof-of-Stake consensus mechanism, where validators stake ETH as part of the network's security model.

ETH can also be transferred between users and used by applications operating on Ethereum.

8. What Is Gas?

Gas is the unit used to measure the computational work required to perform operations on Ethereum.

Transactions require computational resources, and users pay fees for those resources using ETH.

More complicated operations can require more computational work and therefore more gas.

Gas is an important part of Ethereum's design because it helps prevent unlimited or abusive computation from consuming all network resources.

9. What Is Gwei?

Ethereum fees are commonly expressed in gwei.

Gwei is a denomination of ETH.

One gwei equals one billionth of one ETH:

1 gwei = 0.000000001 ETH

Using gwei makes very small transaction costs easier to read and communicate.

10. How Does Ethereum Reach Agreement?

Ethereum currently uses a Proof-of-Stake (PoS) consensus mechanism.

Validators participate in the consensus process by staking ETH and running validator software.

Validators check proposed blocks and participate in confirming the state of the network.

The system includes rewards for appropriate participation and penalties for certain forms of dishonest or seriously improper behavior.

11. What Is an Ethereum Validator?

A validator is a participant that helps secure Ethereum's Proof-of-Stake network.

Under Ethereum's current protocol, a user operating a full validator deposits 32 ETH into the staking deposit contract and runs the required software components.

Validators check blocks, participate in attestations and may be selected to propose blocks.

The 32 ETH figure applies to the protocol's standard solo-validator deposit requirement. Other staking arrangements can provide different ways for users to participate economically.

12. What Happened to Ethereum Mining?

Ethereum previously used Proof-of-Work.

In September 2022, Ethereum completed its transition to Proof-of-Stake in an event commonly known as The Merge.

Ethereum's Mainnet is now secured through Proof-of-Stake rather than mining.

Therefore, descriptions of Ethereum as a network secured by Proof-of-Work are historical rather than current.

13. What Is an Ethereum Account?

Ethereum uses accounts to represent entities that can hold assets and initiate transactions.

There are two broad categories: externally owned accounts controlled by private keys and contract accounts controlled by smart contract code.

A user's wallet normally provides an interface for managing keys and interacting with Ethereum accounts.

14. What Is a Transaction on Ethereum?

A transaction is a signed request that can change Ethereum's state.

It may transfer ETH, interact with a smart contract or perform another operation supported by the protocol.

Transactions consume computational resources and therefore require gas.

The transaction must be valid according to Ethereum's protocol rules before it can be included in the blockchain.

15. What Is the Ethereum Blockchain?

The Ethereum blockchain is the ordered history of blocks that have been accepted by the network.

Blocks contain transactions and other information needed by the protocol.

Each block references its parent, creating a linked sequence of blocks.

Ethereum nodes use the protocol's consensus and execution rules to maintain agreement about the current state of the network.

16. What Are Decentralized Applications?

Decentralized applications, often called dapps, are applications that use blockchain-based infrastructure.

On Ethereum, smart contracts can provide programmable components while user interfaces can allow people to interact with those contracts.

Examples can include financial applications, marketplaces, games, token systems and other services.

The degree of decentralization can vary significantly between applications. An application using Ethereum does not automatically mean that every part of the application is decentralized.

17. What Are Tokens on Ethereum?

Ethereum allows developers to create digital tokens through smart contracts.

Tokens can represent different types of digital assets or rights, depending on how they are designed and used.

Common Ethereum token standards include ERC-20 for fungible tokens and ERC-721 for non-fungible tokens.

A token created on Ethereum is not the same thing as ETH. ETH is Ethereum's native asset, while tokens are created through smart contracts operating on the network.

18. What Is Staking?

Staking involves committing ETH to Ethereum's Proof-of-Stake consensus system.

Validators stake ETH as collateral while participating in network consensus.

Staking can involve technical, operational and financial risks. Rewards are not guaranteed to be a particular amount, and different staking arrangements have different conditions and risks.

19. What Are Ethereum's Main Limitations?

Ethereum has important technical and economic limitations.

Network capacity is finite. When demand for block space increases, users can face higher transaction fees.

Smart contracts can contain programming errors, and applications built on Ethereum can introduce additional risks.

Ethereum's ecosystem also includes wallets, bridges, exchanges, oracles and other infrastructure. Each additional component can create its own technical or operational risks.

20. Ethereum Is Not an Investment Promise

Ethereum is a technological network, while ETH is a market-traded digital asset.

The usefulness of the Ethereum network does not guarantee a specific market price for ETH.

Market prices are influenced by supply and demand, liquidity, expectations, economic conditions, regulation, technology and many other factors.

Conclusion: Ethereum as a Programmable Network

Ethereum extends blockchain technology beyond the simple transfer of a native digital asset.

Its architecture combines a distributed blockchain, the Ethereum Virtual Machine, smart contracts, gas-based computation and Proof-of-Stake consensus.

ETH is the native asset of the network and plays an important role in transaction fees and network security.

Understanding Ethereum therefore requires looking beyond its market price and examining the technology, economic incentives, applications and risks that make up the ecosystem.
