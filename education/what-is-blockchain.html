What Is Blockchain?

How Distributed Ledgers Work

A beginner-friendly guide to blocks, consensus, decentralization, security and the technology behind digital assets.

Education · Blockchain · Technology · Digital Assets

1. What Is a Blockchain?

A blockchain is a type of distributed ledger: a system for recording information in a way that can be shared and independently verified by multiple participants in a network.

The word “blockchain” describes one important design feature. Information is grouped into blocks, and blocks are linked together in a chronological sequence using cryptographic techniques.

A blockchain is therefore more than simply a database. Its design combines data structures, cryptography, networking and a consensus mechanism that determines how participants agree on the state of the ledger.

2. Why Was Blockchain Important for Bitcoin?

Bitcoin introduced a system for transferring value electronically without requiring a central institution to maintain the transaction ledger.

The Bitcoin white paper described a peer-to-peer electronic cash system in which transactions could be recorded in a public chain of proof based on computational work.

This design addressed an important problem in digital payments: how participants can prevent the same digital unit from being spent more than once without relying on a central clearing authority.

3. What Is a Block?

A block is a collection of records that has been accepted into a blockchain according to that network’s rules.

Depending on the blockchain, a block can contain transaction data, information about the previous block, a timestamp or other metadata, and information associated with the network's consensus mechanism.

The exact structure is different from one blockchain to another. Bitcoin, Ethereum and other networks do not all use identical block formats or operate according to identical rules.

4. How Are Blocks Linked?

Cryptographic hashing is one of the technologies used to connect blocks.

A cryptographic hash function produces a fixed-length value from input data. A small change in the input produces a different hash.

In a blockchain, information relating to an earlier block can be incorporated into the data of a later block. This creates a cryptographically linked history.

The result is a structure in which changing historical data can require changes to subsequent data and, depending on the consensus system, overcoming additional network protections.

5. What Does Decentralization Mean?

Decentralization does not mean that a blockchain has no rules or no infrastructure.

It generally refers to the distribution of important functions among multiple independent participants rather than placing complete control in one central institution.

Different blockchains have different degrees and forms of decentralization. The number of participants, their geographical distribution, the concentration of infrastructure and the design of governance can all affect how a network operates.

6. What Is a Distributed Ledger?

A distributed ledger is a record that is maintained across multiple participants or systems rather than being stored and controlled by one central database administrator.

Participants can maintain copies of the ledger and use the network's rules to determine which updates are valid.

Blockchain is one way of implementing a distributed ledger, but not every distributed ledger is necessarily a blockchain.

7. What Is Consensus?

A blockchain network needs a method for determining which proposed changes to the ledger should be accepted.

This is the role of a consensus mechanism.

Different networks use different approaches. Bitcoin uses Proof-of-Work, while Ethereum currently uses Proof-of-Stake.

These systems have different technical designs, economic incentives and security assumptions. There is therefore no single universal blockchain consensus model.

8. Proof-of-Work

In Bitcoin's Proof-of-Work system, miners compete to produce blocks by performing computational work.

The process requires electricity and specialized computing equipment. The network's rules make it computationally expensive to rewrite established transaction history.

Bitcoin's design combines this computational cost with economic incentives and network verification rules.

9. Proof-of-Stake

Proof-of-Stake uses a different model.

On Ethereum, validators participate in the consensus process by staking ETH and following the network's rules. The protocol includes mechanisms that can penalize certain forms of dishonest or inappropriate validator behavior.

Proof-of-Stake does not mean that a blockchain is automatically centralized or decentralized. Its actual characteristics depend on the protocol design and how participation is distributed.

10. Is Blockchain Immutable?

Blockchain is often described as “immutable,” but this word should be understood carefully.

A blockchain does not make information physically impossible to change. Instead, its technical and economic design can make unauthorized alteration of established records difficult and detectable.

The strength of this property depends on the particular network, its consensus mechanism, the number and independence of its participants, and the circumstances being considered.

11. Are Blockchains Completely Anonymous?

Public blockchains are generally better described as transparent or pseudonymous rather than completely anonymous.

On a public blockchain, transaction information and addresses may be visible to anyone who can access the network's ledger.

An address does not necessarily reveal the real-world identity of its owner by itself. However, blockchain analysis and information from other sources can sometimes connect addresses with entities or individuals.

12. Smart Contracts

Some blockchains support programs that execute according to predefined rules. These are commonly called smart contracts.

Ethereum was designed as a programmable blockchain and provides an environment in which smart contracts can operate.

Smart contracts can support decentralized applications, token systems, financial protocols and other forms of programmable digital interaction.

A smart contract is not a human legal contract simply because it has the word “contract” in its name. Its legal meaning depends on the surrounding facts and applicable law.

13. What Can Blockchain Be Used For?

Blockchain technology can be used for different types of digital recordkeeping and coordination.

Examples include cryptocurrency networks, tokenized assets, decentralized applications, digital identity experiments, supply chain systems and other forms of shared databases.

However, the existence of a blockchain does not automatically make a particular application useful, secure or economically efficient. Each use case must be evaluated according to its own technical, economic and legal characteristics.

14. Blockchain and Digital Assets

Blockchain technology provides infrastructure for many digital assets.

Bitcoin is the native asset of the Bitcoin network. ETH is the native asset of Ethereum and is used within the Ethereum ecosystem, including for paying transaction fees and participating in Proof-of-Stake consensus.

Other networks have their own native assets and technical designs. Tokens can also be created on existing blockchains according to the rules of those platforms.

15. What Are the Main Limitations?

Blockchain technology has important limitations.

Networks can face trade-offs involving scalability, transaction costs, speed, security, decentralization, energy consumption and usability.

Different blockchains make different technical choices. A design that improves one characteristic can sometimes create costs or constraints elsewhere.

Blockchain systems can also depend on software, network infrastructure, wallets, exchanges, bridges, oracles and other components that may introduce additional risks.

16. Blockchain Is a Technology, Not an Investment Promise

The existence of a blockchain or cryptocurrency does not guarantee that a particular asset will increase in value.

Technology, market price and investment risk are separate questions. A technically interesting project can still have substantial financial, operational, regulatory or security risks.

Understanding the technology is therefore only one part of understanding a digital asset.

Conclusion: Understand the Infrastructure

Blockchain is a technological approach to maintaining and coordinating records across a network of participants.

Its significance comes from the combination of distributed networking, cryptography, data structures and consensus mechanisms.

Bitcoin demonstrated one important application of this architecture: a decentralized monetary network. Ethereum expanded the concept toward a programmable blockchain capable of running smart contracts and decentralized applications.

The technology continues to develop, but the fundamental questions remain important: Who maintains the network? How are transactions validated? What incentives exist? What can participants verify? What are the risks and limitations?

Understanding those questions is a better starting point than simply looking at the price of a digital asset.
